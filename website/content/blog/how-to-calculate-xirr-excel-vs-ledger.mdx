If you search "how to calculate XIRR" you'll find dozens of tutorials. Almost all of them tell you the same thing: download your transactions, make two columns in Excel (Date, Amount), enter your stock buys as negative and your portfolio value as positive, and run `=XIRR()`.

That method works. But it gives you the wrong number.

Not because the formula is wrong — XIRR is XIRR. But because **the data going into it is incomplete.**

Here's what every Excel tutorial misses.

## The Excel Approach (How Most People Do It)

Most investors who try to calculate XIRR do something like this:

**Step 1:** Open a spreadsheet. Create two columns: Date and Amount.

**Step 2:** Enter every stock purchase as a negative cash flow:

```
01-Jan-2023   -10,000   (bought HDFC Bank)
15-Feb-2023   -8,500    (bought Infosys)
10-Apr-2023   -12,000   (bought Reliance)
...and so on
```

**Step 3:** Add the current portfolio value as a positive:

```
02-Mar-2026   +65,000   (portfolio value today)
```

**Step 4:** Run `=XIRR(amounts, dates)` and get a number like 18.3%.

This looks right. You put in ₹30,500 over time, your portfolio is ₹65,000 today. 18.3% sounds plausible.

**But here's what you didn't include.**

## What You're Missing: Every Rupee the Broker and Government Took

Every single trade you made came with a set of charges. These weren't optional. They were deducted silently from your account, directly from your ledger balance.

### Brokerage
Zerodha charges ₹20 per executed order (flat fee model). If you've made 100 buy orders over 3 years, that's ₹2,000 in brokerage before you've even counted the other charges. Active traders making 5–10 trades a month: ₹1,200–₹2,400/year in brokerage alone.

### STT — Securities Transaction Tax
This is the big one. For delivery trades (stocks you hold overnight), STT is 0.1% on both buy and sell value. On ₹10,000 worth of stock:
- Buy side STT: ₹10
- Sell side STT: ₹10
- Total: ₹20 per round trip

For a ₹5 lakh portfolio with normal churn, STT alone can be ₹1,000–₹5,000 per year.

### Exchange Transaction Charges
NSE charges approximately 0.00322% on equity delivery. Small number — but it's there on every trade and it adds up across years.

### GST (18% on brokerage + exchange charges)
The government taxes your transaction costs. On ₹20 brokerage + ₹5 exchange charges, you pay ₹4.50 in GST. Per trade. Every trade.

### Stamp Duty
0.015% on the buy value for delivery trades. On ₹10,000: ₹1.50. Not huge per trade — but across 3 years of investing, it's real money leaving your account.

### SEBI Turnover Fees
₹10 per crore of turnover. Tiny, but present.

**Let's add this up for a realistic example.**

Assume you've made 80 trades over 3 years, averaging ₹15,000 per trade. Total turnover: ₹12,00,000.

| Charge | Estimated Amount |
|---|---|
| Brokerage (80 trades × ₹20) | ₹1,600 |
| STT (0.1% buy + 0.1% sell × ₹12L) | ₹2,400 |
| Exchange charges (~0.003%) | ₹360 |
| GST (18% on brokerage + exchange) | ₹353 |
| Stamp duty (0.015% on buys) | ₹900 |
| **Total charges** | **≈ ₹5,613** |

That's **₹5,613 that left your account and never went into a stock.** It reduced your returns directly.

If you only tracked stock purchases and sales in your Excel sheet, **you never counted these as an investment cost.** Your XIRR calculation is pretending these charges didn't happen.

The real XIRR is lower. How much lower depends on your trading activity, but for most retail investors: **0.5% to 1.5% per year in missed costs.**

That gap compounds. Over 10 years, 1% per year means your "real" XIRR might be 14% when your spreadsheet says 15%. That's not a small rounding error — it changes your entire read on whether your stock-picking is actually working.

## The Ledger Method: Why It's More Accurate

Your broker keeps a ledger. It's the master record of every rupee that moved in or out of your account.

Every fund transfer in: recorded. Every fund transfer out: recorded. Every charge: recorded as a debit. Every STT payment: recorded. Every payout: recorded.

The ledger doesn't care about your individual stock positions. It just tracks money.

When you download your Zerodha ledger (CSV), Groww balance statement (PDF), or Fyers ledger (CSV), you get a complete record of:

- All your deposits (exact dates, exact amounts)
- All your withdrawals (exact dates, exact amounts)
- All charges already deducted (they show up as negative balance movements)

**The key insight: you don't need to add charges separately. They're already gone from the ledger balance.**

When you calculate XIRR from ledger cash flows:
- The deposits are your actual outflows — including all the money that went to charges
- The withdrawals are your actual inflows
- Your current portfolio value is what's left

**The XIRR from this data is your true net return after every cost.**

## Why Zerodha and Groww Don't Show This Automatically

You might wonder: why don't the apps just show the right XIRR?

The honest answer is that it's a design choice. Showing a lower-but-accurate number is less appealing than showing a higher-but-simplified number. A 19% XIRR on the app looks better than a 17.4% "true after-cost XIRR."

There's also a technical reason: XIRR calculation requires defining a terminal value — the current portfolio worth as of today, entered by you. The app shows live market value, but the XIRR formula needs you to declare the date you're measuring to. It can't do that without your input.

So the app shows approximations. The ledger shows the truth.

## How to Do It Right

### Option 1: Excel (Manual)

1. Download your Zerodha Fund Statement (All Segments → CSV)
2. Open in Excel. Look for rows where the "Particulars" column says "Funds transferred" or "UPI" — these are your actual deposits. Find all withdrawal rows too.
3. Create a Date/Amount table with deposits as negative and withdrawals as positive
4. Add today's portfolio value as a positive amount on today's date
5. Run `=XIRR(amounts_column, dates_column)`

This takes 45–90 minutes the first time. You have to know which rows to include and which to ignore.
